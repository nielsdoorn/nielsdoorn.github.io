On Friday 13 November 2026 at 16:00, I defended my dissertation _O bug, where art thou? Improving software testing education_ at the [Open Universiteit](https://ou.nl) in Heerlen. And yes, despite worldwide MS Teams outages and train services disruptions, Friday the 13th turned out to be a lucky day: I can now call myself a PhD!

The following is a short summary of my presentation and my thesis.

## Not done until it is tested

Students should not consider their programming work done before they have tested it properly. But we do not teach them to test, and they do not like testing. That, in one sentence, is the problem my dissertation addresses.

### CS students aren't taught to test

Testing is under-represented in computer science curricula, and students graduate unable to design and evaluate tests. They do not find testing attractive. They see it as something that happens after implementation, they test to confirm what they hope works rather than to find what fails, and they confuse testing with debugging. Industry does not fill the gap either: most testing expertise is acquired on the job, and the communication, teamwork and critical thinking skills employers ask for are rarely taught.

Knowing how to test is the baseline, and we largely fail to provide it in education. But knowing how is not enough: students have to *want* to test. I like to call this being *test obsessed*, after Fowler's "test infected" (a term that got a different flavour during COVID). A student who finds testing genuinely interesting keeps doing it when nobody is marking it.

### From rationalist construction to empirical inquiry

My research asked how we can teach computer science students to test. It looked at how students make sense of test case design, how testing can be integrated into early programming education, and how empirical learning activities can enhance students' sensemaking, critical thinking and engagement in testing.

**Evidence and diagnosis**

- A **systematic literature review** showed that testing education remains fragmented and that soft skills are neglected.
- A **sensemaking taxonomy** describes how students actually approach testing. Most students take a *developer approach*: they treat testing as a programming task and follow the code rather than the specification. *Convergent testers* apply known techniques systematically, but close down rather than explore. *Divergent testers*, the goal, reason empirically and explore the unknown to generate new knowledge about the system. The taxonomy gives educators a diagnostic framework: identify where students are, design interventions, and measure movement toward divergent testing.

**Interventions**

- **TILE (Test Informed Learning with Examples)** introduces testing from day one by embedding it into existing programming exercises, without a curriculum redesign. Testing introduced too late becomes testing never learned. Only 3 of 10 widely used introductory programming textbooks contain anything TILE-like, and half do not even introduce `assert`. The exercises are freely available in an open repository.
- **Puzzle-based learning** trains five cognitive dimensions of a critical tester: experimenting, knowledge curation, communicating, dispositions, and handling emotions under pressure. The puzzles have been used with vocational, bachelor, master and PhD students as well as industry practitioners.
- **BugOutbreak** is a serious game that combines testing tours and Socratic questioning into collaborative gameplay. Players explore, form hypotheses, gather evidence and reason about risk.

TILE creates exposure; puzzles and games create depth. Both are needed.

### Why this matters beyond testing

Software quality is worsening, with code shipping with defects, vulnerabilities and performance problems. Testing education cultivates critical evaluation, systematic experimentation and reasoning under uncertainty, which are exactly the skills that responsible use of GenAI demands. Curiosity, tolerance for ambiguity and reasoning from incomplete evidence transfer well beyond testing. Critical thinking is no longer only a CS competence.

My main conclusion: students aren't ignorant of testing, they are applying the wrong paradigm. Software testing education needs a shift from rationalist construction to empirical inquiry, and with the right pedagogy, students can learn to think more like testers.
